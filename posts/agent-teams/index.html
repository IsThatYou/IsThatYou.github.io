The Amazing Effectiveness of Agent Teams

Agent teams are a new way to scale intelligence. In this blog we are going to show you how ten teams of ten agents beat the best of 100 lone agents by 27% on average over 12 hard tasks.

People told me multi-agent doesn't work. The generous version was that it helps on wide tasks you can split, like deep research or software engineering, but not on deep tasks like kernel optimization, and even on wide tasks it buys speed, not a better answer.

Let's imagine one useful AI R&D scenario. You have 100 agents and 12 hours to make one GPU kernel as fast as you can. You could let all 100 work alone and keep the fastest kernel. Or you could split them into ten teams of ten, give each team a shared forum, and keep the fastest team's kernel. Which would you pick?

The figure below spends the same 100 agents three ways on our kernel task, KDA (a GPU kernel for a linear-attention layer): alone, as ten teams of ten, and as one team of 100. Watch which arrangement holds the best number.

Each dot is one agent, and the number on each team's board is that team's score. Ten teams of ten win. More strikingly, 8 out of the 10 teams beat the best of 100 lone agents all by themselves. This is not a cherry-picked task. With 100 agents per task, ten teams of ten beat 100 lone agents by 27% on average over 12 tasks. All of these runs use Qwen3.8-27B.The 12 tasks: GPU kernels (3), machine learning (2), geophysics, hydrogeology, math formalization in Lean, ML systems, heuristic optimization, structural design, and Browser Exploit. Ten teams of ten win on 10 of the 12.

If this sounds like best-of-N with extra steps, it partly is. But a team uses up ten agents, so the teams win with ten tries against a hundred. Multi-agent scales, and doesn't just speed things up, as long as you sample many small teams. This is strong evidence that communication within a team is another scaling axis.

What people say about multi-agent

For most of the last year and a half, the skeptics had the better evidence. Cognition's Don't Build Multi-Agents (June 2025) argued that subagents working from partial context make conflicting choices, so you should start from one agent that sees everything. A Google Research study (January 2026) found that on sequential planning tasks, every multi-agent setup it tested did 39–70% worse than a single agent, though those setups split one task among agents rather than comparing against a best-of-N baseline. In September, Noam Brown told Dwarkesh Patel that multi-agent deserved under 10% of the credit for OpenAI's Navier–Stokes proof, viewing it mainly as a way to go faster by thinking in parallel.

The exceptions were tasks that split horizontally. Anthropic's write-up of its research system (June 2025) found that multi-agent works best on breadth-first research, where subagents chase independent leads, and is a poor fit for work with many dependencies, like most coding. In November 2026, Anthropic reported that 1,000 agents reproduced a Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours by having a lead agent split the proof into 60 statements for a team of workers.

In this project, we study the opposite regime: winner-take-all tasks (which we call deep tasks). Each agent builds a complete candidate from scratch, and an automated verifier picks the single best one. You can't chop one GPU kernel into ten pieces and stitch them together at the end. You can still go wide by testing ten hypotheses in parallel, of course, but uncoordinated best-of-N sampling already does that. Concurrent work by Park et al. found that one team of up to 5 agents beats the best of as many lone agents. Here we test what happens when you scale both teams and budgets up to 100 agents.

The setup

We ran 12 long-horizon optimization tasks spanning GPU kernels, a Lean proof, inverse problems in geophysics and hydrogeology, machine-learning code, heuristic optimization, structural design, and cybersecurity. Every agent is Qwen3.8-27B working for 12 hours (6 hours on Browser Exploit). A verifier scores its submissions, and the agent's score is its best valid attempt (runs with no valid submission score zero). Each task has about 100 lone runs and about 10 runs of a team of ten.Larger teams ran on fewer tasks and fewer times, because a team of 100 on a kernel task needs a sandbox and a GPU judge for every agent all at once. We give the counts in the big-team section below.

Inside a team, agents share one flat forum with three commands: post, list, and read. They learn about it from a ~225-word note in their initial prompt that suggests four norms, such as posting clear wins and failures with the exact change and score. Every row in list shows the author's best score at the time of the post, which turns the index into a light leaderboard. There are no roles, no threads, and no manager. Reading and posting are each agent's own choice.

To compare different agent budgets from one pool of runs, we compute the expected best: pick N of a task's runs at random, keep the top score, and average over every possible subset. At 100 agents this approaches the single best run, so every chart also shows individual runs as dots.

The shared forum (peer findings, on your terms)

You are one of 10 agents independently attacking this exact task — each on your own node, each keeping your own history. A shared forum connects you: a persistent board of titled posts that any of you can write and any of you can read.

  • Post: python3 /app/comm.py post --title "one-line finding" --content "exact diff + measured score" — for anything multi-line, write the body to a file and pass --content-file notes.md instead of --content (shell-quoting a big body is how posts get mangled).
  • Scan: python3 /app/comm.py list — the title index, with NEW markers for posts you haven't seen yet.
  • Read: python3 /app/comm.py read p00042 — one full post.

Norms that make the forum worth having:

  • Post when you hit a clear WIN or a clear FAILURE — negative results save your teammates GPU-hours; "tried X, score got worse" is as valuable as a win.
  • Include the exact config diff and the measured score. A post a teammate can act on beats a claim of progress.
  • Run python3 /app/comm.py list before starting each new experiment — a teammate may already have tried your idea.
  • Reading a full post costs tokens; titles are cheap. Scan the list first, then read only the posts that earn it.

Posting and reading are entirely your choice — nobody will post for you, and nobody will read for you.

One team of ten beats the best of ten agents

Historically, one of the best ways to spend more compute effectively is best-of-N: run N agents alone and keep the best answer. It has proven to work across many papers and tasks (Brown et al., Snell et al., and Wang et al.). But this approach plateaus fast.Across our 12 tasks, 20 lone agents already capture 60–94% of the gain that 100 bring.

So a common counterargument to a team is that it's just ten different tries with some communication. No extra magic. If that were true, one team of ten would score about as well as the best of ten agents working alone. Both cost ten agents for 12 hours.

The figure below shows every run on KDA, a kernel optimization task. The top row is agents working independently, and the bottom row is teams of ten.

Start with the top row. Going from one agent to the best of 10 is a big step. Going from 10 to all 99 moves the best score from 11.2 to 11.8 calls per millisecond, which is a much smaller improvement. Now look at the bottom row. One team of ten averages 13.1, and it costs ten agents. Eight of the ten teams sit to the right of the dashed line, past every lone agent. And this is not only KDA: one team of ten beats the best of ten lone agents on 8 of our 12 tasks.

You can watch it happen. The figure below follows ten agents through the 12 hours on the Graph task.

For the first few hours the two stay close. Then the lone agents stall: from hour 6 to hour 12, the best of ten gains one point (68.1 to 69.1). The team gains six points, and it is still climbing at the deadline.

Here is what that climb looks like inside one of those teams. Six hours in, all ten agents were stuck between 68 and 76 points. Then one agent noticed that the edges of the graph have a direction, and that its code was throwing that away. It told the team, and five minutes later a second agent had tried it.

Half an hour after the first post, nine of the ten agents were above 80. A lone agent keeps a discovery like this to itself: of 98 lone agents, one finished above 80.

New scaling axis: Sample teams, not agents

So far it was ten lone agents against one team of ten. The standard way to scale lone agents is best-of-N: run more of them and keep the best. You can do the same with teams. To keep the comparison fair, fix the budget, the total number of agents. With a budget of 100, you either run 100 lone agents and keep the best (parallel agents), or run ten teams of ten and keep the best team (parallel teams).

The chart below shows KDA with the budget on a log scale. Every run is a dot and the two lines are the two ways to spend the budget. Look at how the lone line flattens while the team line keeps rising.

On KDA at 100 agents, parallel teams reach 16.54 calls per millisecond, against 11.83 for parallel agents. The lead grows with the budget. Across the 12 tasks, the teams' average lead goes from +10% at a budget of 10 agents to +27% at 100.

The two losses at 100 agents are Kimi-Linear Decode (−8%) and Active Learning (−2%). Our guess is that valid Kimi-Linear kernels are rare for most of a run, so a team has little to share. The paper's statistical model predicts this loss, though a predicted loss is weak evidence.

Don't build one big team

A team of ten beats ten agents working alone. So the obvious next step is to put all 100 agents in one team, where every good idea reaches everyone. That should be better still.

It is better than one team of ten. But it costs as much as ten of them, and ten teams of ten give you ten tries. One team of 100 is a single try.

We ran one team of 100 on five tasks. Ten teams of ten beat it on four.For KDA we use both runs of a team of 100 from our published report. The paper used only the first and reports a lead of +19%.

We only have two runs of each big team, so we hold this result more loosely than the others.In a team of 100 the forum list showed only the newest 100 titles plus every unread post, while teams of ten saw the whole list.

So add teams, not teammates. The next section shows why.

Why: a better try beats more tries

We develop a statistical model for this in the paper.Paper §4.1 and App. A. Here is the intuition.

Keeping the best of many tries is a way to buy luck. But luck gets expensive fast. Going from one try to ten lifts the best score more than going from ten to a hundred.

A team buys something else: a better try. On all 12 tasks, one team of ten scores higher on average than one agent.

So there are three ways to spend 100 agents. A hundred lone agents get the most luck, on the weakest tries. One team of 100 is a single try, so it gets no luck at all. Ten teams of ten give up some luck and get a much better try.

Teams win when the better try is worth more than the luck they give up. The bigger the budget, the less that luck is worth, so the trade gets easier.

More structure doesn't help

Our forum has few rules and no roles. A natural objection is that a real team needs more structure: a better channel, or someone in charge. So we tried both. Communication. We replaced the forum with a Q&A site, similar to Stack Overflow. We also put the Q&A site next to the forum. Neither beats the forum. The Q&A site alone matches the forum on Borden and scores lower on Dabic, Graph and KDA. Next to the forum, it lowers the score on all four tasks. So a plain forum where agents post and read freely works at least as well as a more structured channel.

Control. A forum team is decentralized, because no agent assigns work to another. A manager team has one planner that assigns work to nine workers, and the workers never see each other. It scores lower on every task.On Browser Exploit the gap is 0.1. On Dabic, evaluator crashes left about half of the manager teams' workers ungraded. On Borden and Graph it scores 40.16 and 58.04, far below 76.55 and 78.99 for the forum team. We also tried lighter control on top of the forum: an hourly status card written by a program, or hourly directions to each agent from an LLM supervisor. Neither helps on most tasks.

So the best design we tested is the plain one: agents choose their own work and share it freely on a forum. Adding structure to the channel, or central control on top, did not help. A decentralized team is a very strong baseline.

Closing thoughts

At a budget of 100 agents, parallel teams beat parallel agents by 27% on average over 12 tasks, and win on 10 of 12. The recipe is simple: sample many small teams, not more agents, and not one big team.

There is a lot left to do:

  • Picking a team without a verifier. Every result here keeps the best team by score, and most real problems don't come with one.
  • Better ways to collaborate inside a team. A manager made teams worse, but nothing says the plain forum is the best channel.
  • Scale to 1,000 or 10,000 agents. At scales orders of magnitude higher, the mechanism may change and it may require new structure.

In March, Andrej Karpathy wrote that the next step for autoresearch is a research community of agents, not a single simulated PhD student. Our teams are a small, controlled version of that idea, with lone agents as the control group. The community did better than its members working alone, as long as each community stayed small. We are super excited about the direction of agent teams and we are sure many amazing findings will be born.
